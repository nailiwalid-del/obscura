Obscura · Rust prototype · v0.2

A currency you can spend without being read

On a conventional blockchain, every payment is public and recorded forever. Obscura publishes the opposite: nothing. Not the amounts, not the senders, not the recipients. And its security is built to survive a quantum computer.

▲

Educational prototype, with no security audit. The code is complete and tested end to end, but nothing here has been reviewed by a cryptanalyst. Do not entrust it with anything of value.

For everyone

The problem: digital money is an open book

Bitcoin is often assumed to be anonymous. The opposite is true: every transaction is public and permanent. Anyone can read which address sent how much to which other one, and a little analysis is enough to tie those addresses to people. It is a bank statement posted in the town square.

Obscura starts elsewhere: publish nothing at all. The network only ever sees fingerprints that reveal neither the amount, nor who pays, nor who receives — and it can still verify that the transaction is valid. That is the job of a zero-knowledge proof: demonstrating that a rule holds without showing the data that proves it.

The second goal is to last. Quantum computers would break the signatures used today by very nearly every cryptocurrency. Obscura is built so that this would not be enough to bring it down.

The principle

Defence in depth: two locks, never one

Every security function combines two primitives from independent families of mathematics. Security holds if at least one of the two holds.

FunctionPrimitive 1Primitive 2
Key exchange X25519 elliptic curves ML-KEM-768 FIPS 203 · lattices
Signatures Ed25519 ML-DSA-65 FIPS 204 · both required
Encryption AES-256-GCM XChaCha20-Poly1305 cascaded
Hashing BLAKE3 SHA3-256 concatenated, never truncated

The point is not to stack for the sake of stacking. A cryptanalytic breakthrough rarely strikes two families of mathematics at once: breaking elliptic curves says nothing about lattices, and vice versa. Requiring both signatures makes a transaction invalid the moment one is missing — so an attacker has to break both worlds, not the weaker one.

A decision worth knowing about

Algorithms carry an explicit version on the wire: 0x02 denotes the FIPS versions, the only current one. The pre-FIPS round-3 variant (0x01) is refused by name — the two never coexisted. Version negotiation is an attack surface; a flat refusal is not.

Privacy

What the chain actually holds

The model follows the Zerocash lineage. On chain there are only two kinds of object:

  • commitments (64 bytes) — the fingerprint of a coin whose value and owner stay hidden;
  • nullifiers (32 bytes) — the mark that a coin has been spent, without saying which one.

Amounts, senders, recipients: never published. The recipient finds their coins by scanning the chain with their receiving key, which alone can decrypt the notes addressed to them.

This also explains a constraint further down this page: since the amount is precisely what the encryption hides, no rule can bound a production reward. Hence the absence of a coinbase.

The core

The STARK circuit is not an option: it is the consensus

In many projects the proof is a layer added on top of rules written elsewhere. Here, the circuit statement is the consensus rule. What the circuit proves — seven properties, P1 through P7 — defines what a valid transaction is; there is no second definition it could drift away from.

The circuit is a monolith of variable shape: 1 to 4 inputs and 1 to 4 outputs. A wallet holding a single note can pay, and the consolider command merges scattered notes. The shape used (2-in / 2-out by default) remains public — that point is covered in the threat model.

What is proved, and with what caveat

Since revision 3z-b1 the consensus proof is witness-hiding (HVZK in the random-oracle model). The caveat is explicit and owned: this is the honest-verifier model, on an unaudited prototype. The full argument is in the STARK statement FR.

Consensus

Finality, and what produces it

Obscura does not mine. Blocks are produced in turn by a committee of authorities (at most 64): for a given height and view, the legitimate producer is designated mechanically, and its block is valid only when accompanied by a 2f+1 quorum certificate, with n = 3f+1.

The verification order is not an implementation detail: the certificate is checked before any STARK verification. A badly certified block is rejected without ever paying the cost of a proof.

This is what gives finality: contradicting a certified block would require f+1 participants to sign two blocks at the same height — a provable fault, not a mere divergence. An absent authority is no longer a deadlock: after a delay, the others move to the next view and route around it. The committee itself is reconfigurable on the same chain, a change certified by the quorum taking effect a few blocks later.

The cost, and its permanent bound

The certificate does not aggregate — no post-quantum signature scheme allows it today. Its size therefore grows linearly with committee size: about 1% of the block at 4 authorities, but 13.9% at 64, where a block carries 8 transactions instead of 9. Committee size is thus bounded by the block budget, and that bound is structural.

Honesty

The five known limitations

They are here, in the body of the page, not relegated to a footnote. A prototype that hides its limitations cannot be evaluated.

Sealing authority is federated, not decentralised

Block production belongs to a list of authorities burned into the genesis, now reconfigurable on the same chain. A genesis without authorities yields an open chain, where ordering is agreed among cooperative participants but never defended: fine for a local testnet, not for a public network. Reducing the committee to three members or fewer sacrifices fault tolerance — the quorum then equals the whole committee, and no absence is tolerated.

The node serving history learns a great deal

It sees the wallet's IP address, the cadence of its requests and its position in the chain. Withholding an output let it present a perfectly coherent chain in which the omitted payment stayed invisible.

The --temoin (witness) option queries a second node at the same height and compares roots: a disagreement halts everything before application. Three caveats remain — the witness is only worth anything if chosen independently (two nodes run by the same operator count as one, and the protocol cannot check this); a disagreement does not say which of the two is lying; and the option is off by default.

Money is created only at genesis

The consensus rule admits no nuance: above height zero, no issuance. That is what prevents inflation from being propagated and accepted. A chain therefore boots from a parameterised initial block, and its money supply is fixed once and for all. Rewarding a producer would first require a rule that bounds the amount issued — and that amount is precisely what the encryption hides.

The stated soundness is 78 bits

The verifier demands a proved bound, not a conjectured one that would yield a flattering figure for the same proof. 78 bits is a prototype level: it would need raising before any serious use, at the cost of larger proofs.

The post-quantum backend debt is open and acknowledged

The whole family of libraries supplying the post-quantum primitives is marked unmaintained upstream, including the FIPS variants. Evaluating the alternatives concluded not to migrate now: none is better than the status quo, and “unmaintained” is not “vulnerable”. The trigger criteria are written down in advance, to be re-read before freezing any public genesis.

A structural consequence

No reorganisation is possible. State is append-only throughout. Supporting reorganisations would mean redesigning the ledger — not adding a function.
