Method · case study

How a p = 0.019 becomes a p = 0.39

Obscura separates what is proved from what is assumed. That is easy to write down. Here is what the discipline looks like when it costs us a result we wanted to keep — on a different project, under the same rules.

The result that was not there

Three campaigns, two machines, one significant deviation

An information-theoretic quantity Ξ has, for a qubit, an analytic maximum at η* = 0.5643766. The experimental question was simple: does the measured peak land there?

Three campaigns — two on IBM Marrakesh at 8192 shots, one on IQM Garnet at 512. X/Y/Z tomography, density-matrix reconstruction, a 10,000-iteration binomial bootstrap of the raw counts, then inverse-variance meta-analysis.

Combined result: 0.5586666, 95% CI [0.5539090, 0.5634242]. The theoretical value falls outside. p = 0.019. Zero heterogeneity across campaigns. A small deviation — clean, and reproduced on two independent machines.

Why it was tempting

A consistent deviation across platforms does not have many comfortable explanations: either physics the model did not contain, or a hardware calibration drift. A dedicated campaign was ready to test the second. It ran out of credits halfway — and that interruption is what forced a cold re-reading of the analysis.

The mistake

The estimator followed one curve; we tested it against another

The estimator for Ξ consumes the reference state as measured. That state is not pure: its Bloch radius is 0.983 in the first IBM campaign.

The value 0.5643766, on the other hand, assumes a perfectly pure reference. For radius x < 1 the path gives Ξ(η) = (1−η) x artanh(ηx), whose maximum sits elsewhere — by an amount that is fully computable before any measurement.

Reference impurity accounts for 63% of the shift on its own; the residual, −0.0021, sits well inside the combined standard error (0.0024).

So I do not claim to have explained the shift to the last decimal. I claim there is nothing significant left to explain — a weaker statement, and a sufficient one.

Three signs readable earlier

What a clean pipeline does not protect you from

◈

The residual had a sign

Negative across all three campaigns, at every value of η. When a residual's mean equals its RMS, there is no noise there — only bias.

◈

The bootstrap could not see it

It resampled shot noise, the only source we had modelled. It tightened the intervals around a biased centre — the worst of both worlds.

◈

The follow-up was underpowered

At 512 shots, 1 σ on a Bloch component is 0.044. The drift we were hunting sat below that threshold. The campaign could not have settled anything.

The rule

And why it applies to a protocol too

Rule

The null hypothesis must be computed under the same conditions as the estimator. If the estimator consumes measured quantities, the null must consume them too.

Testing a measured-input estimator against an ideal-input prediction manufactures significance. And the smaller the error bars, the more convincing the false positive: rigour downstream protects nothing if the comparison point upstream is wrong. It makes things worse.

This is the same reason the STARK statement sorts its claims into three registers — proved, assumed, out of model — and why the proved register is replayed by a command rather than asserted:

verify the claims
./verification/verifier.sh --complet

Seventeen claims, exit code 1 if any one of them falls. On its very first run this harness found that eight of the relevant tests were #[ignore]d in debug builds: an auditor running the obvious cargo test would have seen a green suite without those claims ever being evaluated.
