Applied cryptography · Two projects · 2026

Two projects, one question: what does a guarantee rest on?

A payment system promises privacy; a consensus promises it cannot be fooled. Those promises always rest on something scarce and hard to forge. Obscura bets on mathematics that would survive a quantum computer. QPoL asks whether a physical constraint could play the same role.

▲

Nothing here is a product. Obscura is an educational prototype with no security audit — do not entrust it with anything of value. QPoL is research: no published code, no network.

The projects

Two very different stages of maturity

One compiles and runs today; the other is research with nothing published. That asymmetry is shown, not smoothed over.

Obscura

A digital currency where amounts, senders and recipients are never published — and whose security rests on two independent families of mathematics.

  • Public code, in Rust, tested end to end
  • Complete BFT consensus — finality, liveness, reconfigurable committee
  • Runs locally: four binaries, a single-machine testnet
  • Unaudited prototype — no public chain is open

QPoL

Research: what if the scarce resource protecting a consensus were neither energy nor capital, but a physical constraint that no budget can work around?

  • Theory, simulator and prior-art review carried out
  • No published code, no network, no token

The common thread

A guarantee is worth exactly what its scarce resource is worth

Every decentralised system ends up answering the same question: what stops someone from cheating? The answer is always a resource that cannot be manufactured at will. Mining answers “energy”, and production concentrates wherever electricity is cheap. Proof of stake answers “capital”, and concentration follows wealth.

The two projects here attack that same point from opposite ends. Obscura takes the classical resource — mathematical hardness — and asks what survives a quantum computer: the answer is one rule, every security function rests on two primitives from independent families, and the whole holds if at least one holds. QPoL asks whether a physical resource could replace them.

They share no code and do not depend on one another.
